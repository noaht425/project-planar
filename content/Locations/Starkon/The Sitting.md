The entrance examination of the [[Aravis Academy]], held once a year in [[Hekatania]] over the last days of Mortera. Everyone calls it the Sitting.

Anyone may sit it. There is no fee to sit and no requirement to have studied anything, and a candidate who has never seen a spellbook can score top marks.

Nothing in it requires magic, and no magic is permitted in the hall. This is the point of the whole exercise. The first year assumes nothing, so the examination assumes nothing either, and what it measures is whether a person can be taught.

Tens of thousands sit. A few thousand are taken. A candidate is given a numbered token at the door, is called by the number and never by name, and goes through six stations in whatever order the invigilators send them.

## The Six Stations

**The Figure.** A card is held up for a count of ten and taken away. It carries a figure of nine strokes. The candidate draws it.

What is marked is the number of strokes and the order they went down in, which the invigilator watches rather than infers. Proportion is not marked at all, and a wobbling, ugly, correct figure scores above a handsome one with eight strokes in it. A sigil with the right shape and the wrong count is a different sigil, and the Academy would rather find that out now.

**The Fault.** The candidate is given a written procedure, a table of objects, and no explanation, and is told to carry out the instructions exactly.

The instructions are wrong. At the sixth step they call for something that is not on the table and cannot be made from what is. A candidate who forces the procedure through to the end scores nothing. A candidate who stops and says so scores. A candidate who stops, says so, and proposes the correction scores highest.

Every candidate in Hekatania has heard that there is a planted error, so there are two. One is harmless and can be worked around. Reporting the harmless one and then carrying on past the real one is its own result, and the invigilators have a mark for it.

**The Box.** A closed box sits on the table with something inside it. The candidate may ask the examiner twenty questions that can be answered yes or no, and may not touch the box.

The questions are what is marked. A candidate who names the object in four questions and a candidate who never names it at all can leave with the same score, because the examiner is recording how each question narrowed what was left. Twenty questions that each halve the field is a pass. Twenty guesses at objects is not.

**The Cup.** The candidate holds a full cup at arm's length, counts aloud backwards from three hundred in sevens, and answers the examiner's questions while doing it. The questions are about nothing in particular and must be answered in sentences.

Spilling ends the station. Losing the count ends the station. Falling silent ends the station. Nobody is told which of the three matters most, and the answer is that they are marked separately.

**The Sum.** Four pages of arithmetic, long and tedious and not difficult, with a line at the top of the first page saying that the candidate may stop whenever they like and hand in what they have.

Nothing on the paper says a finished page scores better than an unfinished one, and nothing says it does not. What the station marks is whether the candidate checked any of their own work, and whether they asked the invigilator what the sum was for. Asking scores. Finishing all four pages without asking anything scores about half as well as two pages that have been checked.

**The Question.** One question, asked aloud, that no candidate could possibly know the answer to.

"I do not know" scores. A confident wrong answer scores nothing. A confident wrong answer that the candidate defends when the examiner pushes back scores below nothing, and this is the only station in the Sitting where a candidate can lose marks earned elsewhere.

## The Benches

Candidates wait between stations on the benches in the outer hall, for upwards of four hours in total, with nothing to do.

The benches are marked. Invigilators sit among the candidates dressed as candidates, and what they write down is who helped somebody, who went looking for what was coming next, who copied, who talked themselves into a state, and who simply sat.

No candidate is told this before, during, or after. A fair number of people who were certain they had passed have not, and the Academy has never explained itself to any of them.

## The Marks

The pass mark is not published and moves every year, because the Academy takes the number of students it can actually teach. A score that was taken one year has been turned away the next.

Resitting is allowed and is common. Nothing is held against a candidate who has sat it before, and a professor will occasionally tell a first-year that they failed twice themselves.

The one standing rumour about the Sitting is that one of the six stations is not scored and the Academy will not say which. All six are scored.

> *I sat the Sitting, under a number, to find out what was in it. I will not be publishing my result. What I will say is this. I went into it certain the Question was the trick. I spent the whole day building an answer to a question I could not have answered. When I finally said I did not know it, the examiner wrote something down and looked pleased. I have not stopped thinking about the benches.*

## Running It At The Table

The Sitting is written to be played rather than rolled, and the stations work as they are with no mechanics attached.

**The Figure.** Draw a real nine-stroke figure, show it to the player for ten seconds, and take it away. Count their strokes.

**The Fault.** Hand the player a real procedure with a step that cannot be done. The result is whatever they say out loud.

**The Box.** Think of an object and answer twenty yes-or-no questions honestly.

**The Cup.** Have the player actually count backwards from three hundred in sevens while you ask them about their character's family. No roll is needed and the station is funnier without one.

**The Sum.** Give them a long sum and see whether they ask what it is for.

**The Question.** Ask their character something nobody could know, and see what they do.

If dice are wanted, the stations map to Intelligence for the Figure and the Box, Wisdom for the Fault, Constitution for the Cup, and no check at all for the Question, which should be played straight.

## Design Notes

The examination is built to measure five things the first year will demand: exact copying, knowing when an instruction is wrong, asking questions in a useful order, holding attention under interference, and admitting a limit. None of them require any magic, which is why a farm child from Fernstadt and a noble's son with tutors sit the same paper.

The Benches are the part worth keeping. The Academy watches candidates when they believe they are not being watched, which tells a player something about the institution before they have been admitted to it.

The Question is the one to run slowly. Players will try to answer it.
