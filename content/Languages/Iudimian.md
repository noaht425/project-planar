Language of the [[Omens]] of the [[Kingdom of Krotaphos]]. Rebuilt by the Omens from the [[Old Iudimian]] that was still spoken and from surviving temple texts, with gaps filled from [[Celestial]] and [[Infernal]]. Spoken at home across Krotaphos. Since 427 AM, [[Kingdom of Sylfaen]] has allowed it in public only in religious settings.

> *Twenty-two words in modern Iudimian are wrong. They are wrong because the Omens rebuilt the language from a script they could only half read, and the misreadings stuck. God is Tithon because somebody read a d as a t and nobody has ever been able to put it back. You learn the error and you do not mention it in Krotaphos.*

## Grammar: Sounds
Words that stayed in everyday speech changed in these ways:
- Judgement accents, vowel length marks and nasal vowels were lost. A nasal vowel became a vowel followed by n.
- y became i. ph became f, ch became k, and chth became th.
- At the start of a word, ps, pt, pn, gn, mn and kt lost their first sound, and x became s.
- Long vowels shifted: aa became e, ee became i, ii became ai, oo became u, uu became au.
- At the end of a word, -on became -en, and -a and -o became -e.
- The infinitive ending -ine became -in.

## Grammar: Words From the Texts
- Religious words were learned from temple texts, not from speech, and keep their old endings: Themalon (kingdom), Tithon (god), Anuma (soul).
- The late syllabary does not record accents, vowel length or nasal vowels, and it writes p and b, t and d, k and g, s and z, f and v with one sign each. Scholars read those signs with the unvoiced value, so Dīthon (god) became Tithon and Zȳkon (scales) became Sikon.
- The month names, the names of the six days and the twelve domain nouns were copied from the oldest temple texts, written in the early syllabary, which kept every sound and wrote long vowels twice. They came through unchanged: Blython, Neemon, Veriton, Pavlon, Themon, Gwenon, Belphala, Abala, Tiyala, Lukala, Archala, Lilia.

## Grammar: Borrowings
- Where neither speech nor the texts kept an Old Iudimian word, the Omens borrowed one. Words the angels had sorted as their own came from Celestial, and words the devils had sorted as their own came from Infernal. Borrowed words then went through the same sound changes as native ones.

## Grammar: Synonyms
- Early on, the Omens who leaned toward the angels and those who leaned toward the devils used different words for the same thing. Many of these survived side by side with the native word and took on different shades. A native word is plain, a Celestial word is lofty or approving, and an Infernal word carries contempt or a hunger for power.
- Judgement accents are gone from Modern Iudimian, so choosing among these words is how a speaker shows approval or contempt.

## Grammar: Day and Night
- No Modern Iudimian word keeps the old roots shared with the true names of the Kings of Day and Night. Day is Hemer, night is Nikte, the sun is Elie and the moon is Mene. Today is Hodemer, daily is Hemeral, and "to arise" is now said with Egermin (to wake).

## Grammar: Nouns
- Nouns have no gender and no case endings.
- The plural adds -i. A final -e becomes -i: Korpe, Korpi (body, bodies).
- The possessive adds -s after a vowel and -es after a consonant: Korpes (of the body), Ommenes (of the eye).
- A few nouns keep their old plurals:

## Grammar: Comparison
- The comparative adds -ter- and the superlative adds -tam- inside the joined adjective, before its last part: Dossik (glorious), Dosseterik (more glorious), Dossetamik (most glorious).
- The thing compared against follows Dem: Dosseterik dem Krise (more glorious than gold).

## Grammar: Word Order
- Subject, then verb, then object.
- Adjectives come before the noun in their joined form: Agat korpe (a good body). The old wrap-around adjectives survive in prayers and formal speech.
- Oux (not) stands before the verb in statements. Mie (not) stands before a command or wish.
- Dem means from, as well as about. It took over the work of the lost ablative.
- Xa- keeps its old spelling and sound as a learned prefix, because of its tie to Xal: Xathonik (immortal).

## Grammar: Verbs (Active, Indicative)

Infinitive: -in

Present Tense:
| Person | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | — | -on |
| 2nd | — | -on |
| 3rd | -t | -on |

Past Tense:
| Person | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | -se | -se |
| 2nd | -se | -se |
| 3rd | -se | -se |

- The stem is the infinitive without -in: Esin, es- (to be); Dunain, duna- (to give). After a consonant, the 3rd singular is -et: Eset (is).
- The past ending comes from the old aorist.
- The participle adds -men, from the old result suffix: Dunamen (given).
- The future uses thel, from Thelemin (to want), before the infinitive: Hal thel itemin (they will go).
- The perfect uses ech, from Echemin (to have), before the participle: En ech dunamen (I have given).
- The passive uses es, from Esin (to be), before the participle: Hal es dunamen (it is given).
- The command form is the bare stem: Duna (give).
- The subjunctive, the moods and aspect are gone. Of the old evidentials, only tsi survives, as a word at the end of a sentence meaning "so they say": Hal itemse tsi (they left, so they say).

## Grammar: Numbers
- The Omens reclaimed two. Dix is an ordinary number again, and Dixast means second.
- Everyday counting is in base 10. Zero to six are native: Outhir, Hin, Dix, Tirse, Tesor, Penke, Iudel. Seven to ten and the larger units were borrowed from [[Dzoa]] during the long years under Sylfaen: Bado (7), Tinu (8), Lakwe (9), Zeke (10), Bute (100), Haz (1000), from Dzoa Bhado, Ṭiṇu, Llaqwe, Dzeka, Mbuṭa and Ḥaz.
- The temples still count in sixes, using Orme (36) and Mirre (216).

## Grammar: Idioms
- Hin hemer, dix hemeri. "One day, two days." One more can't hurt. Said when giving in to another helping, another drink or another round.
- Dix themaleteni hedemon. "Two kings are sitting." Both sides have been heard, and the deal is fair.
