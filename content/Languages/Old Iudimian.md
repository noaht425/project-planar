No longer spoken by anyone, ancestor to [[Iudimian]], [[Celestial]], and [[Infernal]]

Spoken by the [[Original Judgements]] from their creation around 10,000 AF until [[The Division]] in 3,097 AF, when [[Celestial]] and [[Infernal]] began to split from it. [[Iudimian]] was later rebuilt by the [[Omens]] from what was still spoken and from surviving texts, with gaps filled from Celestial and Infernal. Religious words and the most basic everyday words survive best. Much of the rest is reconstructed or unknown.

> *Three marks, and the whole of my first term went into them. The acute approves, the grave condemns, the macron does neither, and they sit on a syllable whose position depends on the gender of the noun. I once wrote a line of praise for a tutor and handed in a formal denunciation of him, correctly spelled.*

## Grammar: Gender
- Angelic (nominative singular ending: -on)
- Demonic (nominative singular ending: -a)
- Neuter (no fixed ending; each Neuter word has its own forms)

Gender sorts a word by the domains of the archangels (Angelic) and the archdevils (Demonic). It does not mark sex, and it does not mark good or evil (see Accents). Nearly every word is sorted as Angelic or Demonic. Neuter is rare.

## Grammar: Sounds
Vowels:
- Short: a, e, i, o, u
- Long (written doubled): aa, ee, ii, oo, uu
- y: originally pronounced like French u (as in lune), now read as i
- Nasal: ã, ẽ, ĩ, õ, ũ
- Diphthongs: ai, ei, oi, au, eu, ou
- Two vowels in a row that do not form a diphthong are pronounced separately (Aaranea, Charios)
- A vowel followed by n is not nasal. The Angelic ending -on is o followed by n.

Consonants:
- Stops: p, b, t, d, k, g
- Aspirated stops: ph, th, ch (pronounced as in Attic Greek, p, t and k with a puff of breath)
- Affricates: ts, z (dz)
- Fricatives: f, v, s, sh, h
- Nasals: m, n, gn (as in Italian)
- Liquids: l, r (trilled), lh (as in Italian gli)
- Semivowel: w
- A doubled consonant is long (ll, tt, ss)

Syllables:
- Most syllables are open.
- Allowed clusters include ps, x (ks), pt, kt, mn, sp, st, sk, and a stop followed by r or l (tr, pl, chr). These clusters can begin a word.

## Grammar: Stress
- Stress can fall on any syllable. Each word's stress is learned with the word and marked in its dictionary entry.
- Heavy syllables attract stress. A syllable is heavy if it contains a long vowel, a diphthong or a nasal vowel. A syllable ending in a consonant is not heavy unless its vowel is.
- Gender pulls stress. In Angelic words, stress moves from its dictionary position to the nearest heavy syllable before it. In Demonic words, stress moves to the nearest heavy syllable after it. If there is no such syllable, stress stays where it is.
- Adjectives have no gender, so gender does not pull their stress (see Adjectives).
- In Neuter words, stress stays in its dictionary position.
- In verbs, if the stressed syllable is light and a heavy syllable sits directly next to it, stress moves to the heavy syllable. If heavy syllables sit on both sides, stress moves to the later one.

## Grammar: Accents
- The accent sits on the stressed syllable and moves with it when the word inflects.
- The acute accent (á) marks a word as approving. The grave accent (à) marks a word as condemning.
- A word with no judgement, including every Neuter word, takes a macron (ā).
- On a long vowel or a diphthong, the accent goes on the second letter (aá, oí).
- A stressed nasal vowel carries both marks (ã́).
- Judgement and gender are independent. An Angelic word can be condemning and a Demonic word can be approving.

## Grammar: Word Order
- Word order is free. Case endings show each word's role in the sentence.
- Subject pronouns are usually dropped, since the verb ending shows the person. A subject pronoun is used for emphasis.
- A verb with more than one subject is plural.

## Grammar: Number
- Nouns have two numbers: singular and plural. There is no dual.

## Grammar: Cases
- Nominative: subject
- Accusative: direct object
- Genitive: of, belonging to
- Dative: to, for (indirect object)
- Instrumental: by means of, using
- Ablative: from, away from, out of
- Locative: in, at, on
- Vocative: addressing someone
- Permutative: in exchange for (anything done, given, received or suffered in exchange for another thing, from trade and prices to reward and punishment)

## Grammar: Verb Categories
- Tense: present, past, future
- Aspect: imperfective (ongoing), aorist (completed as a whole), perfect (completed with a lasting result)
- Voice: active, middle (acting on or for oneself), passive
- Mood: indicative (fact), subjunctive (possibility, purpose), optative (wish), imperative (command)
- Evidentiality: witnessed, reported, inferred
- Person: 1st, 2nd, 3rd
- Number: singular, plural
- Verbs do not change for the gender of their subject.
- Prayers, oaths and statements of faith use the witnessed evidential.

Restrictions:
- Only the indicative marks tense. The subjunctive, optative and imperative mark aspect only.
- Evidentiality appears only in the indicative.
- The indicative has seven tense and aspect pairings: present imperfective, present perfect, past imperfective, past aorist, past perfect, future, and future perfect.
- The imperative has 2nd and 3rd person forms only.
- Endings are fused. Each allowed combination of tense, aspect, voice, mood and evidentiality has its own set of person and number endings.

## Grammar: Word Shape
- Approving verbs tend to end in a vowel (give, help, heal).
- Condemning verbs tend to end in a consonant cluster or x (hurt, interfere).
- Verbs with no clear judgement (die) tend to end in a single soft consonant, such as -em.
- Nouns do not follow these tendencies. A noun's judgement is shown only by its accent.

## Grammar: Verb Formation
- The dictionary form is the active infinitive, ending in -ine. The middle infinitive ends in -inei and the passive infinitive in -ineur.
- Primary endings (present and future): -ax, -she, -tor, -kal, -aito, -ont.
- Secondary endings (past): the primary endings with their last letter removed: -a, -sh, -to, -ka, -ait, -on.
- Tense and aspect markers stand between the stem and the ending. Present perfect -ka-, past aorist -sa-, past perfect -ke-, future -se-, future perfect -xe-. In the subjunctive, optative and imperative, the aorist takes -sa- and the perfect takes -ka-.
- Subjunctive: the primary endings with their first vowel doubled.
- Optative: -eu- followed by the secondary endings.
- Imperative: -o (2nd singular), -oth (3rd singular), -othe (2nd plural), -onth (3rd plural).
- The middle voice adds -i to the end of the ending. The passive voice adds -ur.
- Evidentiality is added last. Reported adds -tsi and inferred adds -her. After a consonant, a comes before the suffix (-axatsi, -axaher).
- The tables below list every complete ending.

## Grammar: Reduplication
- Every perfect form reduplicates the stem. This covers the present perfect, past perfect and future perfect, and the perfect aspect of the subjunctive, optative and imperative.
- Copy the stem's first consonant and first vowel onto the front of the stem.
- If the stem begins with a stop followed by r or l, copy only the stop and the first vowel.
- If the stem begins with any other cluster or with a vowel, double the stem's first vowel instead.

## Grammar: Junctions
- When a stem ending in a consonant meets a marker or ending that begins with a consonant, e is inserted between them.
- When two vowels meet, they are written together. Two identical vowels form a long vowel, and the listed pairs form diphthongs.
- A long vowel followed by the same vowel stays long (aa + a = aa).

## Grammar: Numbers
- Old Iudimian counts in base 6.
- Number words do not decline.
- A number is said from its largest place to its smallest, one word per place: Mȳrre (216), Ōrme (36), Iūdel (6), then the units.
- A place with a multiplier above one puts the multiplier first: Tīrse Iūdel (18).
- A place with a multiplier of one uses the place word alone: Iūdel Hín (7), not Hín Iūdel Hín.
- Empty places are skipped: Ōrme Hín (37).
- Dỳx is used only for exactly two things. In every compound number and in counting aloud, a 2 in any place is said as Hīnatirse: Iūdel Hīnatirse (8), Hīnatirse Iūdel (12).
- Numbers above 1295 use a number as the multiplier of Mȳrre: Iūdel Mȳrre (1296).

| 20 | 32 | Tīrse Iūdel Hīnatirse |

## Grammar: Ordinals
- Ordinals are formed with -ast. A final vowel drops before the suffix: Tīrse, Tīrsast (third).

- In a compound number, only the last word takes -ast: Iūdel Hínast (seventh).

## Grammar: Fractions
- A fraction adds -mir to the denominator. With a numerator of one, the denominator stands alone: Tīrsemir (a third), Iūdelmir (a sixth).
- A larger numerator comes first as a separate word: Pēnko Iūdelmir (five sixths).
- Fractions do not decline.
- There is no word for a half. A half is said as three sixths, Tīrse Iūdelmir. No fraction is built on Dỳx or Hīnatirse as a denominator.
- Two thirds is said as four sixths, Tēsor Iūdelmir.
- Fractions are most often given in sixths and thirty-sixths: Iūdel Tīrse Ōrmemir (nine thirty-sixths, a quarter).

## Grammar: Pronouns
- Pronouns decline in every case. 1st and 2nd person pronouns have no gender. Only the 2nd person has a vocative, which matches its nominative.
- 3rd person pronouns are built on H- with the noun endings of their gender (Hōn, Hūn; Hā, Hārk).
- Neuter nouns take one of two 3rd person pronouns, singular only. Sūri takes the day pronoun Hī with unvoiced endings. Dālle takes the night pronoun Hē with voiced endings. Each new Neuter noun's entry states which of the two it takes.
- A group of people of mixed or unknown gender takes whichever gender the speaker prefers. The choice shows where the speaker's sympathies lie.
- Xāl takes a blended pronoun built on Hāl- with Xāl's own endings, singular and plural.
- Pronouns carry no judgement and take the macron.

## Grammar: Prepositions
- A preposition comes directly before the noun or pronoun it governs.
- Many relations are carried by case alone: the locative for in, at and on, the ablative for from, the dative for to, the instrumental for by means of, and the permutative for in exchange for. Prepositions add relations the cases do not cover.
- A preposition that takes two cases changes meaning with the case. In general, the locative marks a position and the accusative marks motion: Ēpher with the locative is "above", and with the accusative it is "across".
- Prepositions carry no judgement and take the macron.

## Grammar: Conjunctions
- A conjunction stands at the start of the clause or word it joins.
- Doubled, Thē and Ēl make pairs: Thē … Thē (both … and), Ēl … Ēl (either … or).
- Mēchre is a preposition with a noun and a conjunction with a clause.
- Conjunctions carry no judgement and take the macron.

## Grammar: Negation
- Old Iudimian has two words for "not". Oūx negates statements of fact. Mīe negates anything that is not a statement of fact.
- Oūx is used with the indicative.
- Mīe is used with the subjunctive, optative and imperative, with infinitives, in clauses beginning with Eīk (if), and in clauses of purpose.
- A negative stands directly before the word it negates. To negate a whole clause, it stands directly before the verb.
- A command is negated with Mīe and the imperative: Mīe blàxto (do not harm).
- Two negatives in one clause strengthen each other. They do not cancel.
- Negatives carry no judgement and take the macron.

## Grammar: Adjectives
- An adjective has two parts. The first part attaches to the front of the noun and the last part attaches to the end, making one word: Vér- + Ānema + -it = Véranemait (a true soul).
- Adjectives do not change for gender, number or case. The last part attaches after the noun's case ending: Véranemarkit (a true soul, accusative).
- The junction rules apply where the parts meet. Two consonants take an inserted e, and two vowels are written together.
- When more than one adjective modifies a noun, they nest. The adjective most essential to the noun attaches first, and each further adjective wraps the whole word: Mā- + Véranemait + -kel = Māveranemaitekel (a great true soul).
- The finished word has one accent, on the first part of the outermost adjective, carrying that adjective's judgement. The noun's accent and any inner adjective's accent are dropped, and gender does not pull the stress.
- A preposition stays a separate word before the noun: Ādis Véranemarkit (toward a true soul).
- An adjective standing in for a noun ("the deathless ones") uses its joined form with the noun endings of the gender the speaker chooses: Xathōnikeil (of the deathless ones).
- An adjective used without a noun, as in "the soul is true", joins its two parts on their own: Vérit. This joined form is the dictionary form. Each entry gives its parts on a Split line.
- Adverbs attach to verbs the same way. The last part follows the verb's full ending, including any evidential suffix. Oūx and Mīe are separate words and stand before the verb.

## Grammar: Demonstratives
- Demonstratives are adjectives and attach to the noun like any other adjective.
- The last part gives distance: -de for near the speaker (this), -nos for farther off (that).
- The first part gives the speaker's opinion, and its accent matches: Hō- is neutral, Él- is admiring, Ìst- is scornful.
- A demonstrative is always the outermost adjective, so its accent and opinion govern the whole word: Hō- + Sūrint + -de = Hōsurintede (on this day).
- Used alone as a pronoun ("this one"), a demonstrative takes the noun endings of the gender of the thing it points to, attached to its joined form: Hōdeun (this one, Angelic accusative).

| | Near (this) | Far (that) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neutral | Hōde | Hōnos |
| Admiring | Élde | Élnos |
| Scornful | Ìstede | Ìstenos |

## Grammar: Comparison
- The comparative adds -ter to the adjective's first part. The superlative adds -tam. The junction rules apply: Vér- becomes Véreter- (truer) and Véretam- (truest); Mā- becomes Māter- (greater) and Mātam- (greatest).
- The thing compared against is in the ablative: Véreterit Kaūlor (truer than heaven).
- With a superlative, the group is in the genitive plural: Mātamekel Thēmaleil (greatest of kingdoms).
- Equality uses Ēnthe twice: Ēnthe Vérit Ēnthe Kaūlon (as true as heaven).
- Ēnthe also joins clauses: Ēnthe Kaūlile (as in heaven).

## Grammar: Derivation
- Derived words keep the accent of the word they come from unless their entry gives another. New endings follow the normal stress rules for their gender.
- The junction rules apply wherever a suffix meets a stem.

Agent (one who does something):
- Verb stem + -ton (Angelic) or -ta (Demonic). For people, the speaker chooses the gender: Iūdemeton or Iūdemeta (judge).

Place (where something is done or kept):
- Verb or noun stem + -era (Demonic): Iūdemera (court).

Act (the doing of something):
- Verb stem + -sia (Demonic): Krīnesia (the act of sorting).

Result (a thing done or made):
- Verb stem + -mon (Angelic): Poīmemon (a deed).

Quality (from an adjective):
- Joined adjective + -ia (Demonic): Véritia (trueness).

Diminutive:
- -isk- goes before the gender ending, and the noun keeps its gender: Fāniska (shrine, from Fāna), Árdiskon (a small loaf, from Árdon).

Adjective from a noun:
- The noun stem is the first part and -ik is the last part: Kaūl- … -ik (heavenly), Thōn- … -ik (deadly).

Verb from a noun:
- Noun stem + a stem ending that matches the verb's judgement, then -ine. An approving verb adds a vowel, a condemning verb adds x or a cluster, and a neutral verb adds -em: Músthoine (to reward, from Músthon), Àrxine (to curse, from Àrxa, whose stem already ends in x), Zȳkemine (to weigh, from Zȳkon).

## Grammar: Names
- A foreign name is respelled with Old Iudimian sounds. Its own ending is removed and the noun endings of a gender are added: Zeus becomes Zēnon, Hera becomes Hēra, Apollo becomes Apollon.
- The speaker chooses the gender, as with groups of people.
- A name that already ends in -on or -a can keep its form.
- Names take the macron unless the speaker gives them a judgement.

## Grammar: Compounds
- A compound works like an adjective. The head word splits after its first syllable and wraps the bare stem of the word it governs.
- An agent noun wraps its object: Seīseton (shaker) wraps Chthīr- (earth) to make Seīchthireseton (earth-shaker).
- An adjective wraps a noun stem to make a compound adjective: Lām- … -prit (bright) wraps Ōmm- (eye) to make Lāmomm- … -prit (bright-eyed), which then attaches to a noun like any adjective.
- The compound keeps the head word's accent.
- -ath- before the gender ending turns a compound or agent noun into a title: Seīchthiresetathon (the Earth-Shaker).

## Grammar: Negative Prefix
- Xa- negates an adjective or noun, like un- or -less: Xathōnik (deathless), from Thōnik (deadly). The word keeps its own accent.

## Grammar: Quantifiers
- Quantifiers are separate words placed before the noun: Pānte (all), Pōlle (many), Ōlis (few), Ālthe (other), Tīne (some), Hēkas (each).
- Quantifiers do not decline.

## Grammar: Place Adverbs
- Place adverbs are separate words built on the demonstrative parts. The middle sound gives distance (d near, n far) and the ending gives direction.

| | Near | Far |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where (at) | Hōdil (here) | Hōnil (there) |
| From | Hōdesk (hence) | Hōnesk (thence) |
| To | Hōdis (hither) | Hōnis (thither) |

- The admiring and scornful first parts can replace Hō-: Éldil (here, in this blessed place), Ìstedil (here, in this wretched place).
- Ālthil means elsewhere or abroad.

## Grammar: Other Adverbs
- An ordinal used as an adverb ("first", "second") stands alone before the verb: Hínast Eīremesaon (they said first).
- "As though" is Ēnthe Eīk (as if), followed by the subjunctive.
